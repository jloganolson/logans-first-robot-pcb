Read the copper in KiCad

Choose Inspect J1 battery pour. Start at the ring around J1.2 and follow the uninterrupted filled area to F1.2 and F2.2. A pour is a sheet of copper cut to shape. Turn off Show filled copper and the sheet disappears, leaving the terminal pads visible. This changes the drawing only.

Choose Inspect Q1 gate trace. Start at U1.5 and follow the thin line around the bends to Q1.4. These four top-layer track segments carry the gate control signal. Switch to Whole layer to see the surrounding copper without net highlighting. Click any pad or track to inspect its net.

In the native PCB Editor, open robot_pdb.kicad_pcb, select F.Cu in Appearance and enable Show zone filled areas. Right-click the copper pad at J1.2 and choose Net Tools → Highlight Net. Repeat at Q1.4. Use View → 3D Viewer for the board surface and available component models. The layer view exposes copper that solder mask and component bodies cover on the assembled board. What are Q1, F1 and F2 doing here?

Q identifies a transistor. Q1 is a MOSFET, an electrically controlled switch. Its source and drain carry the logic-branch supply current. U1 drives the small gate trace to turn the channel on for forward power and turns it off when current would flow backward. Q2 and Q3 switch power in the Thor voltage converter. The letter tells you the family, while the surrounding circuit tells you the job.

Follow J1.2 to the fork. Motor current passes through F1 (30 A) on its way to J2. Converter current passes through F2 (20 A) on its way to Q1. The four holes at one end of F1 all grip the same blade terminal. There are two fuse elements on this board. FB1 and FB2 are ferrite beads for filtering noise. A fuse by a battery serves a different stretch of wiring: a short in a pack lead before J1 can draw current without going through either board fuse.

Would two battery inputs isolate the packs? 
Pack A (+) ── fuse ── contact A ──┐
                                  ├── VBAT ── F1 / F2 branches
Pack B (+) ── fuse ── contact B ──┘

This is a proposed topology with common battery negatives. One handle can open both independent contacts. With both open, the positive pack-to-pack path is broken. A switch after the join turns off the robot while leaving the packs connected to each other. When both contacts close, a higher-voltage pack can charge its lower-voltage neighbor through the join, even with the robot idle.

Moving the junction onto the PCB changes connector and copper requirements. It provides isolation only if you add switching or reverse-current blocking ahead of the join. For isolation while parked, an external two-pole DC disconnect can do that job with the present J1. For isolation while running, consider a reverse-blocking input for each pack and design a separate charging and motor-regeneration path. Q1 is downstream of the join and only on the logic branch.

Before choosing hardware, confirm parallel use and BMS reconnect behavior for these packs, operation when one pack drops out, and switch DC interruption and inrush ratings at 33.6 V full.
